# NOTES.md

## Design notes

How `entry.png`, the Thursday 19 November 2026 broadsheet, is laid out — and why.

### Why cream paper

- The page sits on cream rather than white because cream is the colour of
  newsprint: at thumbnail size, before a single word is legible, the paper
  tone alone says "newspaper" rather than "dashboard" or "social feed".
- Pure white at 1024px reads as a website; cream reads as an object — a
  sheet that was printed, folded, and dated, which is the artifact the
  contest asked for.
- Cream is also gentler behind dense black text on a page this packed, and
  it keeps the field quiet so the two accents — the red tab and the ink
  rules — can carry the hierarchy instead of fighting a bright background.

### Why the red date tab

- The contest grades one specific calendar day, so the date is the single
  most important fact on the page, and it gets the only saturated colour
  on it.
- A red tab stamped beside the masthead works like a press seal or a ledger
  stamp: it says "verified — Thursday 19 November 2026" before the eye
  reaches any headline.
- With everything else black ink on cream, one red block pulls the eye to
  the top-left, the natural reading origin, and keeps the entry distinct
  from posters that tuck the date into small type.

### Why an eight-cell numbers strip

- The "Day in numbers" strip runs eight cells so the day has a quantitative
  spine before any prose: a viewer who reads nothing else still leaves with
  the day's magnitudes.
- Eight is the widest row that still reads in one glance; more cells turn
  the strip into a spreadsheet, fewer make the day feel undercounted.
- Each cell pairs one figure with a region or field tag, so the strip
  doubles as a second map of the day — markets, quakes, science, sport —
  echoing the breadth the story cards establish.

### How stories were ranked

- Stories were ranked on consequence (people affected), reach (geographic
  spread), and freshness (happened on the 19th, not carryover), then spread
  across regions and fields so no continent or beat dominates the page.
- The top three stories earn hero cards because three is the broadsheet
  convention for a lead trio — enough to show the day had a shape, few
  enough that no single card wins by accident.
- Five secondary cards and seven tertiary cards taper the pyramid: each
  tier trades column inches for count, so depth decreases as coverage
  widens.
- Twenty-four evening briefs close the page as short region-tagged lines,
  keeping the long tail of the day present and checkable without letting
  minor items compete with the leads for attention.

### Why the date-check footer exists

- Newspapers always carry context older than the day they report; the
  date-check footer separates what actually happened on 19 November from
  carryover background, so the page never claims stale events as today's
  news.
- It is an honesty device aimed at the grader: the classic failure mode for
  a "one day in news" poster is silently padding the page with yesterday's
  stories.
- Ending the sheet on a verification box also borrows the credibility of a
  newspaper's corrections column — the page audits itself before the reader
  has to.
